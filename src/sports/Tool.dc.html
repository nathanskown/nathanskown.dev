The house model

Applies to every league

Each team gets one number: how many points (goals, runs) better or worse than an average team it would be on a neutral field. It is solved from every completed game this season at once, so beating good teams counts more than beating bad ones, and the home team’s built-in edge is taken out first.

Early in a season a few games say little, so every rating is pulled toward a starting point as if the team had already played k games at that level. The starting point is a share of the team’s rating from last season wherever replaying past seasons showed that last year carries information, otherwise the league average. That is the same idea as “SRS × games ÷ (games + k)”. Where it tested better, recent games count more than old ones (a half-life in weeks), blowouts are damped, and teams outside the division the site covers (FCS, non-D-I) share one pooled rating. All-star and international exhibition games are left out, and a relocated team keeps its history (Arizona → Utah).

Ratings come from margins, so they carry a team’s usual quarterback but cannot see a change. For NFL games the projection is moved by the starting quarterback’s recent play (EPA per dropback, recent seasons weighted more, shrunk toward replacement level) against the team’s usual starter, using free nflverse data. NFL ratings also count each game’s result 60% on team efficiency (expected points added per play, from nflverse) and 40% on the final margin, which tested better than margins alone. Fitted on 12 seasons, the quarterback adjustment improved held-out log loss in every one of the last seven test seasons. Betting markets know more still, so the pipeline records the market’s number for every game it forecasts but never uses it: forecasts, ratings and season odds come from scores and this quarterback signal only. After the games finish, the track record compares the house forecast with the market’s, and the gaps show what information is still missing. The market numbers themselves are never published on this site.

NBA projections also count who is playing. Every player gets a value in points of margin, learned only from earlier games’ ESPN box scores: his expected minutes times a blend of his box-score production per minute (game score: points, shooting, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks, turnovers) and his shrunk on/off plus-minus. A finished game’s result is adjusted for the rotation players who sat it, so a loss without a star does not drag the team’s rating as if it were at full strength, and an upcoming game is moved by the value of players on ESPN’s injury report (Out counts fully, Doubtful mostly, Questionable and Day-To-Day partly). Tested on four held-out seasons, this lowered NBA log loss by about 1.6% in every test season and closed about a quarter of the gap to closing lines.

NFL projections count the other 21 positions the same way: a team’s regulars are the players who took at least 60% of its offensive or defensive snaps over its last three games (nflverse snap counts). Results are adjusted for regulars who sat, and upcoming games are moved by regulars on ESPN’s injury report, about one point per missing every-down starter. It improved held-out log loss in nine of the last ten test seasons, a smaller and noisier gain than the quarterback signal.

College football projections count starting quarterbacks: each passer is rated on adjusted yards per dropback from ESPN box scores (touchdowns, interceptions and sacks counted), shrunk toward a below-average level when his record is short. Results are adjusted for who actually started; upcoming games use each team’s latest starter, because there is no free college injury feed. Scored the same way on three held-out seasons, it improved log loss in each, by a small margin.

College basketball preseason ratings account for roster turnover. Each player’s value last season is his box-score production (game score above a replacement level) per team game. A team’s starting rating moves by 0.3 times the value of its current roster, wherever those players were last season, minus the value its own players produced. That way returning stars, departures and transfers all count. Rosters come from ESPN until a team has played three games, then from its box scores. On two held-out seasons it improved log loss in both, mostly in November and December.

College football preseason ratings use returning production, the idea behind ESPN’s SP+. Each player’s share of his team’s passing, rushing and receiving, and defensive production last season is credited to his current team, so departures and transfers both count. Skill-position production carries the most weight. It improved held-out log loss in each of three test seasons. Both college leagues and college hockey also blend in program history: ratings from two and three seasons back pull last season’s rating a little toward them, as KenPom and SP+ do.

In the NHL and both college leagues, each win probability is also blended with a FiveThirtyEight-style Elo rating (20% in college, 30% in the NHL). Elo moves after every game by a margin-of-victory multiplier and regresses toward average between seasons. It reacts to recent results differently from the main ratings, so the two make different mistakes, and the blend scored better on held-out seasons. The blend did not clear the bar in the NFL or NBA, so those leagues don’t use it.

Two further steps passed the same held-out tests. NBA projections are blended with lineup strength, the summed pre-game value of the players expected to play (injury-report players counted by how likely they are to sit); it lowered held-out log loss in each of four test seasons. College basketball margins are stretched by a factor fitted on earlier seasons, because ratings pulled toward a starting point came out compressed (actual margins ran about 1.2 times the projected ones); it cut the held-out margin miss in every test season and leaves win chances essentially unchanged. NBA, NHL, college basketball and college football game pages also show a projected total from offense and defense ratings; checked on the newest complete season they miss by a little more than the closing total, so treat them as context.

NFL game pages add player stat projections and a projected total. A player projection is volume times efficiency: a receiver’s share of his team’s targets times the team’s pass attempts times his yards per target (catch rate for receptions), a runner’s share of carries times team carries times yards per carry, and a quarterback’s share of attempts times yards per attempt. Shares and rates weight recent games most, are pulled toward position averages, and are adjusted for how many yards the opponent allows. The median and middle 50% are not guesses: they are the projection times how players with projections of that size actually finished in earlier seasons, so a sportsbook line can be turned into a measured over/under rate. Game totals come from separate offense and defense point ratings plus wind. Tested on past seasons, prop medians miss by about 23 receiving yards, 1.7 catches, 26 rushing yards and 63 passing yards, and totals miss by about 10.7 points against 10.4 for the closing line; picking overs and unders against the line from these numbers has been break-even, so treat them as a second opinion. Game pages also show each player’s ceiling (the 90th percentile) and the chance of reaching a few round numbers. The Track record page scores the whole range, not just the middle: it counts how often results finished above the stated 75th, 90th and 95th and in each tenth of the range, and lists how the picks did against closing prices by size of gap, overs versus unders, each stat and the latest 50 and 200. We also tested giving each player his own range (from his past games, from his yards per target, and from a simulation of volume times efficiency). Each scored worse on held-out seasons than one shared range per projection size, so the shared ranges stay. NHL game pages show each skater’s expected shots on goal and the chance of 1+ through 5+ shots: shots per minute times expected ice time, adjusted for the opponent, with the chances read from how often players with projections that size really finished with each count over the last three seasons. On held-out seasons that beat a plain minutes-only baseline and matched the actual 1+ to 5+ rates within about a point. The Methodology page has a price calculator that removes the book’s cut and shows break-even and expected return.

The settings that are not obvious are not guessed. The pipeline stores 10 to 12 past regular seasons per league (NFL back to 1999), replays them week by week, projecting each week using only earlier games, and keeps the settings with the lowest log loss. The check is rolling: for each of the four newest seasons the settings are fit only on the seasons before it and scored on that season, which they never saw. They replace the preset values only if they win across those held-out seasons by a clear, statistically tested margin and no held-out season gets clearly worse. Pandemic seasons (2020–21) are left out of fitting. The NFL home edge is the exception: game results are too noisy to pin it down, so it comes from the average closing line over the last five seasons (about 1.6 points, the same for division games since 2021). Lines are used only for that and for checking the model; the site never shows them. Model history shows what each ingredient was worth, season by season.

Three extra inputs are used only in the leagues where the same held-out test showed they help. Rest: each extra day of rest one team has over the other (capped at a few days) is worth a fitted amount, taken out of past results and added to projections; it tested useful in the NFL, NBA, NHL and college football and basketball. MLB starting pitchers: each announced starter gets a rating from the parts of run prevention a pitcher controls (strikeouts, walks, hit batters and home runs per inning), from this season and last, pulled toward average until he has pitched enough innings; the gap between the two starters, scaled to the share of the game a starter usually pitches, is added to the projection. NHL expected goals: goals are noisy, so each past game’s rating input mixes the real goal margin with the expected-goals margin (the quality of the chances each team created); this made held-out NHL predictions better in all four test seasons.

A game projection is the rating gap plus the home edge, plus any rest and starting-pitcher edge. The win probability treats the final margin as normally distributed around that projection: P(home wins) = Φ((home − away + home edge) ÷ s). In leagues where it tested better, s is the league’s σ widened by how unsure both ratings still are, so early-season and thin-schedule games get less confident probabilities; elsewhere s = σ.

Season odds replay the rest of the season 10,000 times. Each simulation draws every team’s true strength from its rating and uncertainty, lets NFL strengths drift a little week to week (injuries, form), plays the remaining games, seeds the playoff bracket and plays it out. Replaying 2003–2025 NFL seasons from weeks 4, 8 and 12, teams given 90% or more made the playoffs about 93–96% of the time and teams given 10% or less about 1–5%. NFL tiebreakers are simplified to division record, then point margin. The NHL simulation plays each team’s remaining games against a league-average opponent instead of the real schedule.

The Track record page logs the model’s pick for every NFL, college football, NHL, NBA and college basketball and hockey game before it starts, freezes it at kickoff and grades it after the final. It begins empty at launch and is not back-filled. The backtest beside it replays past games using only earlier results; it is labelled separately because we ran it after the fact. MLB is not logged. Model history replays how the model improved over past seasons.

What it leaves out: injuries outside the NBA, weather, lineup news other than MLB starters, NFL and NBA availability, and anything else a betting market knows. The gains from the extra inputs are real but small (each lowers held-out log loss by roughly 0.1–0.3%). Treat it as a well-calibrated baseline, not a forecast to bet on.

Data sources

Scores, schedules, box scores, standings, team lists, injury reports and stat leaders come from ESPN’s public site API. NFL seasons 1999–2013 and NFL closing lines come from nflverse (CC-BY 4.0). MLB probable starters and pitching lines come from the MLB Stats API (© MLB Advanced Media, used for non-commercial purposes). NHL expected goals are from MoneyPuck.com. Live pages read it through this site’s own cache (about 30 seconds for scores); standings, injuries and ratings are rebuilt hourly by a scheduled job, and team lists and leaders daily. College football results switch to CollegeFootballData automatically when an API key is configured.

Not shown or used in projections: betting lines (NFL closing lines only set the home edge and check the model), ESPN’s own win probabilities and predictions, and ESPN’s written recaps or injury notes.
