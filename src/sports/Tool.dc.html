The house model

Applies to every league

Each team gets one number: how many points (goals, runs) better or worse than an average team it would be on a neutral field. It is solved from every completed game this season at once, so beating good teams counts more than beating bad ones, and the home team’s built-in edge is taken out first.

Early in a season a few games say little, so every rating is pulled toward a starting point (average, or for the NBA, 65% of last season’s margin) as if the team had already played k games at that level. That is the same idea as “SRS × games ÷ (games + k)”. College football margins are capped so a 60-point win over a weak opponent counts like a 35-point win, and teams outside the division the site covers (FCS, non-D-I) share one pooled rating.

A game projection is the rating gap plus the home edge. The win probability treats the final margin as normally distributed around that projection with the league’s spread (σ below): P(home wins) = Φ((home − away + home edge) ÷ σ).

Season odds replay the rest of the season 10,000 times. Each simulation draws every team’s true strength from its rating and uncertainty, plays the remaining games, seeds the playoff bracket and plays it out. NFL tiebreakers are simplified to division record, then point margin. The NHL simulation plays each team’s remaining games against a league-average opponent instead of the real schedule.

What it leaves out: injuries, rest, weather, lineup news and anything a betting market knows. Treat it as a well-calibrated baseline, not a forecast to bet on.

Data sources

Scores, schedules, box scores, standings, team lists, injury reports and stat leaders come from ESPN’s public site API. Live pages read it through this site’s own cache (about 30 seconds for scores); standings, injuries and ratings are rebuilt hourly by a scheduled job, and team lists and leaders daily. College football results switch to CollegeFootballData automatically when an API key is configured.

Not used: betting lines, ESPN’s own win probabilities and predictions, and ESPN’s written recaps or injury notes.
